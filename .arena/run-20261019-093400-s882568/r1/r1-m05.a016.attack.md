ATTACK 1 [MAJOR] Path A's ad stop-rule keeps loss-making SKUs running, and its budget never reaches the stop trigger
Where: Part 4, Path A: "Only launch SKUs ... with a contribution margin of at least 30% after all of these." and "Ad budget: start at ₹500 a day for 14 days on 3–5 hero SKUs. Stop any SKU whose ROAS ... is below 2.5x after ₹7,000 of spend."
Problem: Break-even ROAS is 1 / contribution margin. At the plan's own 30% minimum margin that is 3.33x, not 2.5x. Counterexample: a SKU at 30% margin and 2.6x ROAS on ₹7,000 of spend brings in ₹18,200 of revenue and ₹5,460 of margin. That is a loss of ₹1,540, and the rule says keep it. The rule only breaks even at 40% margin. The budget is also inconsistent with the trigger. ₹500 a day for 14 days is ₹7,000 in total, split across 3–5 SKUs, so each SKU gets about ₹1,400–2,333. No SKU ever reaches the "₹7,000 of spend" check. As written, the 14-day test cannot kill anything. Path A is billed as the "strongest" path, and this is the one exact money rule it gives.

ATTACK 2 [MAJOR] GST advice is wrong for the plan's own recommended wrapper (an existing family entity)
Where: Part 1 row 14: "Start with services under the threshold. Register GST ... once turnover nears ₹15 lakh". Part 3 Step 1: "If the family already has a company or LLP, run the new line inside it as a separate cost centre". Path A runs inside the family business by definition.
Problem: GST registration and the ₹20 lakh threshold work on aggregate turnover per PAN, not per "cost centre". A family worth about $8M that owns an operating business is almost certainly already GST-registered and far above the threshold. So under Path A, and under the "existing entity" option, every invoice from day one must carry GST: 18% on the Path B services, so the ₹25,000 Starter package is billed at ₹29,500. Returns have to be filed in the entity's GSTIN, and B2C marketplace sales carry TCS. The plan's prices, its margin maths and its "register later at ₹15 lakh" step are all wrong for the wrapper it recommends first. The same applies if the parent's existing proprietorship is reused, because it shares the parent's PAN.

ATTACK 3 [MAJOR] The "near tax-free" ownership-track income fails in the study-abroad scenario the plan itself assumes
Where: Part 6 table: "Under the FY2025-26 new-regime rules, normal income up to ₹12 lakh carries effectively nil tax because of the s.87A rebate". Assumptions: "The student may go to university abroad (US or UK) in August 2028."
Problem: The s.87A rebate is available only to resident individuals. A student who leaves in August 2028 for a 3–4 year degree will normally spend fewer than 182 days a year in India and become a non-resident from FY2028-29 onward. That is the same year the ₹1.5 crore gift is made. A non-resident gets no 87A rebate, so ₹10.5 lakh of interest is taxed at slab rates (or with TDS on NRO interest at about 30% plus cess). Resident FDs and savings must also be redesignated as NRO accounts under FEMA. The headline claim, "job-replacing income at 18 with a single gift ... close to tax-free", is false for the main scenario the plan builds Step 7 around. The plan never mentions residential status.

ATTACK 4 [MAJOR] Path B sells data systems to coaching classes and clinics while ignoring the DPDP obligations it flags elsewhere
Where: Path B targets "clinics (dental, dermatology, physiotherapy), coaching classes ..." and the Growth package includes "automated appointment reminders, lead capture form to Google Sheets/CRM, simple AI FAQ bot". Part 1 row 8 only says "Avoid consumer products aimed at teenagers".
Problem: Coaching-class leads are mostly under-18 students, so they are children's data that needs verifiable parental consent under the DPDP Act and Rules. Clinic flows handle patients' health details. In both cases the student's business, which legally is the parent's proprietorship, acts as a data processor and stores personal data in its own Google Sheets/CRM. The plan gives no data-processing agreement, no rule on who holds the data, no consent wording for the lead forms, and no deletion step. The substantive DPDP obligations phase in during 2027, inside this plan's own timeline. Liability lands on the parent signatory. The plan treats data risk as a tutoring-only issue, but its default business is exactly where the risk sits.

ATTACK 5 [MINOR] Step 7 assumes the 18th birthday falls in Jun–Jul 2028. For many students in this cohort it does not, and the transfer then breaks.
Where: Part 3 Step 7: "Jun–Jul 2028, the student turns 18" and "start a new sole proprietorship in their own name ... or (b) incorporate a private limited company with the student as director".
Problem: A student who is 16 in DP1 in October 2026 turns 18 at some point between about October 2027 and October 2028. Counterexample: a birthday on 15 September 2028. The student flies out in August 2028 still a minor. They cannot sign the proprietorship's current account, cannot become a director, and cannot sign the operator agreement before leaving. Each of these then has to be done remotely from a country whose visa (as the plan itself notes) restricts business activity. The plan gives no branch for a post-departure birthday, such as pre-signing through the parent or appointing the operator before 18.

ATTACK 6 [MINOR] The Path B profit targets contradict each other and the stated unit economics
Where: Part 3 Step 5: "Goal: hold ₹40,000–80,000 a month in profit". Path B: "6–8 retainers by December 2027 ... roughly ₹50,000–1 lakh a month in profit". Unit economics: "about ₹12,000 a month of profit" per retainer.
Problem: Three different figures are given for the same date, December 2027. By the plan's own unit economics, 6–8 retainers at about ₹12,000 is ₹72,000–96,000, so neither the ₹40,000 floor nor the ₹50,000 floor follows from the numbers given. The user cannot tell which target the quarterly kill-or-continue review should test against. All the figures are also pre-tax profit in the parent's hands, taxed at the parent's marginal rate (likely 30%+ with surcharge for this family). Yet Part 6 compares them with salary as "job-replacing" net income.

ATTACK 7 [MINOR] WhatsApp pricing is described on an outdated model
Where: Path B: "The client pays ... Meta's per-conversation charges."
Problem: Meta moved the WhatsApp Business Platform from per-conversation to per-message (per-template-message) pricing on 1 July 2025. That is before this plan's stated "as of 2025–26" law and pricing baseline. A student who quotes clients cost estimates on the conversation model will misprice the pass-through costs of the Growth package. Reminder-heavy clinic flows send many utility templates per patient, so the error matters for exactly the use case being sold.
