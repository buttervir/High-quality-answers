ATTACK 1 [MAJOR] GST claim is contradicted by the plan's own revenue table, so the Phase 3 margin is overstated
Where: F5 "No GST until turnover passes ₹20 lakh/year, which is beyond the Phase 3 target." and the Money plan column "Age 18–19: Revenue per month ₹2,10,000+"
Problem: GST registration depends on aggregate turnover (gross receipts), not on margin. The plan's own Phase 3 revenue of ₹2,10,000+/month comes to ₹25.2 lakh+/year. That is above the ₹20 lakh threshold, so Phase 3 is not "beyond" the limit: it has to register. Even Month 18 (₹1,42,500 × 12 = ₹17.1 lakh) is already close. Private tutoring is not an exempt educational service, so 18% applies. On ₹25.2 lakh that is about ₹3.8 lakh a year, roughly ₹32,000 a month. If prices cannot rise 18% on top of the already premium ₹1,900–2,500/hr, the "₹1,00,000+" margin falls to about ₹68,000. The headline job-replacing number therefore rests on a false compliance assumption. The line "Raise prices first so your margin stays the same" is also untested, because the plan's own pricing is already flagged as an unverified assumption.

ATTACK 2 [MAJOR] The tax claim "taxed in your own hands / likely nil" does not cover agency income earned while the student is still a minor
Where: F3 "Tutoring income is skill income, so it is taxed as yours"; F4 "your tax is likely nil"; "If tutoring doesn't fit you": the pattern "is what keeps it ... taxed in your own hands (F3)"; Phase 2 runs months 7–18, when the student is 16–17.
Problem: The s.64(1A) proviso protects income from the minor's own "manual work or ... application of his skill, talent or specialised knowledge". From Phase 2 on, the income is the ₹900/hour spread on other people's teaching hours. That is business income from employing others, not the minor's own skill, and there is a real risk it gets clubbed with the higher-earning parent's income. With an $8M net worth, that parent is almost certainly in the top slab with surcharge, so ₹67,500/month could be taxed at around 30%+ instead of nil. The plan states the favourable treatment as fact for the whole agency phase. It only sends the "new Act numbering" question to the CA, and never this question, which is the one that decides the outcome.

ATTACK 3 [MAJOR] Recruiting "strong DP2 students" contradicts the plan's own contract logic
Where: Phase 2 step 1: "IB graduates ... aged 18 or over (they can sign contracts, unlike you)" followed immediately by "Strong DP2 students at other schools."
Problem: DP2 students are typically 17, so they are minors. Under F1, which the plan itself cites, a minor's contract is void. The one-page tutor agreement, including the "commitment not to poach your students for 12 months", cannot be enforced against them. The plan's own reason for choosing 18+ tutors therefore rules out half of its recruiting pool. It also leaves a 17-year-old placing other minors in strangers' homes with no adult employer of record. The user is not told which one-page agreement or what guardian-consent process applies to minor tutors.

ATTACK 4 [MAJOR] No concrete child-safety vetting even though adults are sent to minors' homes
Where: Phase 1 "sessions in the student's home are fine"; Phase 2 recruits adult tutors found "through ... LinkedIn"; step 5 mentions only "the child-safety rules" with no content.
Problem: The task asks for "EXACT DETAILS", but the safety rules are never spelled out. A 16-year-old would be dispatching adult tutors they found online into the homes of children aged 11–15. The plan gives no ID check, no police verification (for example Mumbai Police's online verification service), no reference check, no rule that sessions are online-only or held with a parent present for every age, and no incident or complaint procedure. Its only safeguard covers students "under 14" in the student's own sessions, which leaves 14–15-year-old IGCSE students alone with unvetted adults. Parents choosing a teen-run agency will ask exactly this, and one incident ends the business and creates liability for the guardian who signed. The user cannot act on "the child-safety rules" without guessing what they are.

ATTACK 5 [MINOR] Phase 1 numbers are internally inconsistent
Where: Headline "₹25,000–40,000/month by the end of DP1"; time cap "5 hours a week of tutoring ... about 20 paid hours a month"; exit criterion "8+ paying students"; package "10-session package for ₹15,000".
Problem: The cap allows at most 20 hours × ₹1,500 = ₹30,000, so the ₹40,000 top of the headline range breaks the plan's own "non-negotiable" cap. Also, 8 students sharing 20 hours a month means each student gets 2.5 sessions a month. Each ₹15,000 package then lasts 4 months, and no student gets a weekly session. That is unrealistic for exam tutoring and contradicts the retention and package-renewal logic. Either fewer students are needed, or the hours cap is broken.

ATTACK 6 [MINOR] Phase 3 margin arithmetic does not reach the stated figure, and the Phase 2 time budget is too small
Where: "110 tutor-hours a month at ₹900 margin" giving "₹1,00,000+"; Phase 2 "Budget: 4 hours a week" covering matching, QA ("sit in on 1 recorded session per tutor per month"), billing, marketing and recruiting.
Problem: 110 × ₹900 = ₹99,000, and the plan's own table (110 × ₹1,900 − ₹1,10,000) also gives ₹99,000. Neither reaches ₹1,00,000+, even before GST (Attack 1). On time: 5 tutors × one recorded hour-long session is about 5 hours a month for QA alone, out of a total of about 16–17 hours a month. Matching, billing 75 sessions, chasing package renewals, marketing and the 6-month re-recruiting cycle all have to fit in the remaining roughly 11 hours. The DP2 budget is not credible, and the student will face exactly the IB-grades trade-off the plan claims to protect against.
